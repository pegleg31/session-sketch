Verify It: survey data analysis

Why this type: The skill is to perform a technique, procedure, or calculation with a right and wrong way, and students produce a decision, committed and defended, and students work from the dataset, and the planted error is caught by running it and seeing the result is wrong. Together those describe a session whose center is checking confident output against something real — which is what a Research Lab is.

The runner-up — Compete

Tag it Compete if there is a rival or a real clock — someone wins, or the queue does not wait. Without either, a countdown is decoration.

Two rules that override any score

No rival and no clock, no Compete. A countdown bolted onto a verification activity is decoration — if the audit survives without the timer, it is Research. And if the artifact is for someone else to move through, it is Simulate, not Create: a Create artifact is for the maker, a Simulate artifact is for a learner.

The activity

What students actually do

AI produces output that looks right; students catch it by running it.

AI produces something for survey data analysis that looks right. Teams run it against the dataset and find out that it is not. The working tool is Excel.

How it runs

  1. Teams start with the raw data on survey data analysis — working out what it actually measures and what has to be cleaned before it can be used at all.
  2. It does exactly what the team told it — and only that. Watching their own instructions execute is faster than any feedback a professor could give, because the gaps are suddenly visible.
  3. It quietly fills what they left out from its own training, which hides the omission. The constraint — use nothing I did not tell you — always leaks on the first try, and the dataset is where the leak shows.

They leave with

A verified result on survey data analysis: what AI produced, what happened when it was run, and the specific failure that only execution exposed.

Cleaning is where the judgment lives. Give it five explicit minutes and do not hand teams a pre-cleaned file — the session gets thinner the tidier the data is.

== PHASES (50 / 75 / two-session columns; this brief is written for 75 minutes. Build the design system's 50/75 toggle from the first two columns) ==
1. Set the anchor — 50min: 8 | 75min: 12 | 2-session: 14 — facilitator function: Name the real thing this session verifies against.
     participant task: Open the dataset. Before anything else, name what each field actually measures, what is missing or miscoded, and what you would have to clean before trusting one conclusion from it.   [no unlock word; opening phase]
2. Let AI run — 50min: 10 | 75min: 15 | 2-session: 18 — facilitator function: Get AI's fluent, confident output — before checking anything.
     participant task: Write the procedure as if for someone who knows nothing, then have AI attempt it using only your words.   [unlock word: sweep]
3. Verify against the anchor — 50min: 14 | 75min: 20 | 2-session: 24 — facilitator function: Check every claim against the anchor. Mark what fails.
     participant task: Find where it succeeded by guessing rather than by your instruction. Constrain it against the dataset — use nothing I did not tell you — and run it again.   [unlock word: audit]
4. Build the evidence trail — 50min: 10 | 75min: 16 | 2-session: 20 — facilitator function: Conclusion plus its trail — never a summary.
     participant task: Rewrite the one instruction that leaked most, and note what understanding it was hiding.   [unlock word: trail]
5. Present and reflect — 50min: 8 | 75min: 12 | 2-session: 14 — facilitator function: Conclusion, evidence, and the moment AI was confidently wrong.
     participant task: Show the step it got right for the wrong reason, and the instruction you had to add.   [unlock word: present]

== AI SKILL MOMENTS (what the facilitator watches for) ==
1. Phase 2, when a team accepts fluent output without an anchor behind it -> skill: Prompting for evidence rather than assertion -> facilitator move: Ask them to re-prompt for what that specific sentence rests on. Let the room hear what comes back.
2. Phase 3, the first time the anchor contradicts the claim -> skill: Verifying against something real, not against plausibility -> facilitator move: Put it on the projector. This is the highest-value sixty seconds in the session.
3. Phase 3, when a team treats a confident tone as a credential -> skill: Separating fluency from accuracy -> facilitator move: Ask: how sure does it sound, and how sure should you be? Name the gap out loud.

== ROLES (teams of 4, each with a named deliverable) ==
1. Anchor keeper — owns the written anchor and the claim under test, in one sentence each
2. Prompter — owns the prompt log: every prompt sent, in order
3. Verifier — owns the verdict on each claim and the evidence behind it
4. Reporter — owns the finished artifact: conclusion plus trail

== REFLECTION ==
1. Where was the AI confident and wrong, and what made you check that one?
2. What is the difference between how fluent it sounded and how reliable it was?
3. Name what the AI produced and what you decided. Which of the two mattered more to how this turned out?
4. Where in your own work or study, in the next two weeks, could you use this exact move?
Closing stem, every participant completes aloud or in writing: "The most useful thing I learned about working with AI today was ___, and I could use this when ___."
